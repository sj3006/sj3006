"The entries still shift 39.3% per halving" means the whole matrix is recomputed at step $h$ and at $h/2$, and the worst-changing of those 15 numbers moves by 39.3%.

## Slope and curvature

Fix a sensor $s$ and a fault $k$ and vary the fault magnitude $f$ about zero. The reading traces out a curve $y_s(f)$.

- The **slope** is $\partial y_s/\partial f_k$ at $f = 0$: the tangent to that curve at the healthy process. This is what the entry is meant to be.
- The **curvature** is the bending of the same curve away from that tangent.

The estimate used is a central difference, $[y_s(h) - y_s(-h)]/2h$, which is the slope of the *chord* joining the points at $-h$ and $+h$, not the tangent at the middle. Over a wide interval on a bending curve, the chord and the tangent differ, which is what "contaminated by curvature" is pointing at.

## One thing the sentence gets wrong

Curvature is precisely what a central difference already removes. Because the two offsets are equal and opposite, the second-derivative term cancels by symmetry:

$$\frac{y(h)-y(-h)}{2h} = y'(0) + \frac{h^2}{6}y'''(0) + O(h^4)$$

The leading contamination is the **third** derivative, and the error scales as $h^2$. That is exactly why the shift falls fourfold per halving: $h \to h/2$ gives $h^2 \to h^2/4$. The fourfold behaviour the sentence reports is the evidence that the curvature has cancelled, not that it is being avoided by choosing $h$ well.

So I would reword along these lines:

> The step must be small enough that the difference quotient returns the tangent at the healthy point rather than the slope of a chord across a bending response, and is therefore chosen by iterating across a range. At $h = 0.02$ the entries still shift 39.3\,\% per halving; below $h = 0.01$ they settle and the shift falls fourfold per halving, which is the second-order convergence a central difference should show.

## A caveat on the fourfold claim

The claim is correct, but the statistic quoted does not demonstrate it cleanly. Comparing each estimate with the *previous estimate* gives ratios of 81.4 and then 5.66 below $h = 0.01$, because the difference between two approximations can collapse by accident when their errors happen to coincide.

Measured the right way, against a converged reference at $h = 2.5\times10^{-4}$, the errors fall 4.01, 4.05 and 4.20 per halving — textbook $h^2$. If a reviewer checks the number, the consecutive-shift column is the one that will confuse them. I would quote the error-against-reference ratios instead, or state explicitly that the figures are successive differences.

**What the $1/y_s$ prefactor does.** Without it the three rows would be in incompatible units: radiometric power, square metres and kelvin. Dividing by the healthy reading makes each entry a *fractional* change per unit of fault, so every entry is dimensionless and the rows can be compared. Equivalently $J_{sk} = \partial \ln y_s / \partial f_k$.

**Reading one entry.** $J_{11} = 1.5177$ says that a 1% rise in laser power raises the photodiode signal by about 1.52%. $J_{14} = -1.5177$ says a gas fault of the same index lowers it by the same amount, which is the antiparallel pair the paper is about.

So the columns are comparable in *direction*, which is all the angles use, but not in magnitude: an entry of 1.52 in the power column and one of 1.52 in the gas column do not describe equally severe process excursions.
