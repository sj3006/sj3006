## 1. What a monitoring agent is entitled to conclude

This is the one I would write.

The emerging product category is an AI assistant that reads in-situ monitoring data and tells the operator what went wrong. Your geometry says that for some cause pairs this is not a hard inference problem, it is an impossible one: power and gas sit at $0.00^\circ$, and no classifier, no amount of data and no language model can separate them from melt-pool observables. An unconstrained agent will nevertheless name one, confidently, because naming one is what the training distribution rewards.

So: a certificate layer. Before the agent asserts a cause, the diagnosability geometry at the current noise level partitions the fault set into equivalence classes. The agent is permitted to report the class, and is permitted to narrow within it only when build context or an admissible probe provably separates it. The contribution is not the agent, it is the constraint and the proof that the constraint is tight.

What you compute, with no hardware:

- Generate faulted builds from the twin across the five faults and a range of magnitudes.
- Present the resulting photodiode, camera and pyrometer signals to several frontier models, with and without the certificate layer.
- Measure the rate at which an unconstrained agent asserts a unique cause in cases you can *prove* are ambiguous, and the rate at which the constrained agent's reported class contains the true cause.

That second bullet is a crisp, quotable, slightly uncomfortable result: state-of-the-art models assert a single cause in some large fraction of provably indistinguishable cases. It is cheap to produce, it needs nothing you do not already have, and it is the kind of finding that gets cited by people arguing about AI reliability well outside additive manufacturing.

Risk: a reviewer asks why this needs an agent at all, since a Bayesian estimator would do the inference better. Answer that honestly in the paper rather than dodging it. The estimator does the inference; the agent's job is orchestration across heterogeneous evidence and communication under a hard constraint. The defensible novelty is the constraint plus the benchmark, not the inference.

## 2. How good does a twin have to be, for diagnosis?

Digital twin papers almost never say what fidelity is required, because fidelity is treated as a virtue rather than a specification. Your work can give a task-specific answer, and the answer is counterintuitive and favourable to you.

The claim: a twin is accurate enough for diagnosis if its Jacobian angles preserve the separability verdict, which is a far weaker requirement than predicting melt-pool dimensions accurately. You already have most of the evidence. The model carries roughly 13% depth error and 15.5% width error against the benchmark, its fitted parameters sit at their bounds, and yet the worst pair moves by under half a degree across the full range of the unmeasured conductivity coupling, across all four noise sets, and across all 120 fault-to-variable assignments. The verdict is stable where the predictions are not.

Turn that into a stated criterion: diagnosis requires fidelity in the *directions* of the sensitivity vectors, not in their magnitudes, and a twin can be wrong about absolute melt-pool size while being right about which causes are separable.

This does something valuable beyond the result. Your forward model's mediocrity is currently the paper's biggest vulnerability, the thing §5.2 has to apologise for. This reframing makes it the finding.

## 3. Active diagnosis, and where agency provably stops helping

If two causes are confounded at the current operating point, change the operating point. An agent with the twin as its simulator can plan a probe: a commanded parameter perturbation, a diagnostic scan pattern, a modified segment layout, a deliberate change in the spatial modulation that build context exploits. The objective function is already written in your code, the whitened pairwise angle.

The reason this is more than optimal experiment design with an LLM on top is the counterpart result. The rank bound is structural: melt-pool observables factor through four physical channels, so there are degeneracies no probe can break, however cleverly planned. So the paper says: active probing extends diagnosable reach from $k$ pairs to $m$ pairs, and the remaining $10 - m$ are closed to any sequence of probes by channel counting. An agentic method and a proof of its ceiling, in the same paper.

This pairs naturally with idea 1. The certificate layer says what the agent may claim now; the probe planner says what it could claim after acting; the rank bound says what stays unknowable.

## 4. A twin that knows its own parameters are not identifiable

Your §5.2 already notes something reflexive and rather elegant: Bayesian calibration of this model family reports absorptivity, conductivity and heat capacity correlations of 0.93 to 0.96, which is the pathology the paper studies appearing in the paper's own calibration. Your own calibration has the same problem, which is why you had to freeze stored constants rather than refit.

Make that a method. The same whitened-Jacobian machinery applied to model parameters rather than faults tells you which parameter combinations the data constrains and which are free. A calibration loop that detects its own non-identifiability and reports identified *combinations* instead of individual parameters is a real contribution, and it gives the paper a two-level structure using one piece of mathematics: identifiability of faults given sensors, and identifiability of parameters given data.

## Finding 1: the ten degree floor was never a convention

$R_k = \rho^2\sin^2\psi_k$, so the admissible causes form a cone of half-angle $\arcsin(\sqrt{q}/\rho)$ about the observation, and a pair at mutual angle $D$ is resolvable only if $D > 2\arcsin(\sqrt{q}/\rho)$. Inverting: your ten degree floor is the statement that the whitened signal-to-noise ratio is **28.08**, which at 1% channel noise is a 3.2% power deviation. In the build-context regime the same floor means $\rho = 55.84$.

The same inversion prices every pair. Layer against gas would need $\rho = 868$; power against speed, 398; layer against geometry, 60.6. ## Finding 2: the enlarged observation is whitened with the wrong matrix

This one you should act on. Whitening only makes the geometry mean separability if the noise is isotropic afterwards, which dividing each row by its own standard deviation achieves only for independent observations. Three sensors at one instant are independent by assumption, so **every instantaneous result stands**, Table 1's per-segment columns included.

The enlarged observation is not. Its entries are least-squares coefficients, and $\mathrm{Cov}(\hat c_s) = \sigma_s^2\varepsilon^2 (G'G)^{-1}$ is not diagonal: turn density and support depth give a coefficient correlation of 0.577. `coefficient_sigmas` computes the marginal standard errors correctly, to 1.9e-16, and even documents the regressor correlation. What went wrong is using them as if the coefficients were independent. The correct whitening is the inverse Cholesky factor of $\varepsilon^2(\mathrm{diag}(\sigma^2)\otimes(G'G)^{-1})$, which I verified against per-segment noise put through the same least squares.

## The ceiling is a theorem, not a measurement

Everything the model knows reaches the sensors through four channels, so the Jacobian factors as $J = MC$: $C$ is 4×5 and says how each fault moves each channel, $M$ is 3×4 and says how each observable responds. A probe changes $M$ and can never touch $C$. The factorisation checks against the directly computed Jacobian to 6.8e-5, which is the finite-difference error.

Read the first row of $C$. A power fault raises absorbed power, a gas fault lowers it, neither does anything else, so $C[:,\text{power}] = -C[:,\text{gas}]$ **exactly**, from which $(MC)[:,\text{power}] = -(MC)[:,\text{gas}]$ for every $M$ whatever. The null space of $C$ is one-dimensional and is exactly equal power and gas:

```
power -0.7071   speed 0   layer 0   gas -0.7071   geometry 0
```

with `C @ null` exactly zero. This is a strictly stronger claim than the conference paper's. There the pair was confounded for one suite at one operating point, and §5.2 calls the 0.00° an upper bound a richer gas model would open. Here it is confounded for every suite, at every operating point, in any stack of operating points, and the proof is four entries of a matrix.

The fourth channel is intrinsically weak and no operating point changes that. Rank 4 at four probes is rank in name only. More probes make the angles slightly *worse*, so probe selection matters and probe count does not.

## Why build context escapes a theorem that probing cannot

This looked like a contradiction and resolving it is the useful part. The factorisation assumes the fault magnitudes are the same at every epoch. Build context breaks that assumption rather than enriching $M$: a fault's severity varies across the build along its own profile, so what is observed is $J[s,k]B[j,k]$, and $B[:,\text{power}]$ is not proportional to $B[:,\text{gas}]$ even though the channel columns are.

So the escape route is differential modulation of the faults, not richer observation. **The only thing that separates a power fault from a gas fault is that the two vary differently across a build.** Not instruments, not operating points, not probe schedules. That makes WP6 the complement of the build-context result rather than a competitor, and it is the sentence I would build §1 around.
