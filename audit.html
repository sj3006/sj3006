The short version

Split cleanly in two: everything about the predictive model is solid, and everything about explanation stability needs work before submission.

What holds. MSE, RMSE, MAE, R², the dataset descriptives, the hyperparameters, the predicted RUL for test instance #10 — all reproduce to the digit, across two scikit‑learn minor versions. The six‑feature stable core (time, S15, S11, S4, S13, S3) holds in 250 of 250 independent LIME runs, so the bimodal‑stability interpretation in §4.3 is well founded.

What does not. The reported J̄ = 0.636 is the most pessimistic estimate of LIME’s stability that five runs can produce. Table 6 implies 0.707; the stated seeds give 0.800; the true value over 31,125 pairs is 0.741. Against 0.741, WA‑LIME’s 0.778 is a +5% gain, not +22.3%. And at matched compute, plain LIME with more perturbations beats WA‑LIME outright.

Table 7 is internally consistent — with an experiment that is not Table 6

Table 7’s numbers do hang together. Five runs × 8 slots = 40; six core features occupy 30; if the eight unstable features share the remaining 10 slots with two appearing twice and six once, then exactly 2 of the 10 pairs share a seventh feature:

2 pairs with |Aᵢ ∩ A⬩| = 7J = 7/9 = 0.7778
8 pairs with |Aᵢ ∩ A⬩| = 6J = 6/10 = 0.6000
mean0.6356 → rounds to the reported 0.636

So Table 7 is arithmetically sound. The problem is that Table 6 does not describe that experiment — its union is 11 features, not 14, and only 5 are unstable, not 8.

The prose names features that are not in Table 6

§4.3 says the unstable features are “Sensors 2, 7, 9, 12, 17, 18, 19, and Operational Setting 3” — eight, matching Table 7’s count. Table 6 contains Sensors 2, 7, 8, 12, 14.

Named in prose, absent from Table 6: S9, S17, S18, S19, Operational Setting 3. In Table 6, missing from prose: S8, S14. Over 250 runs, Operational Setting 3 never once enters the top‑8.

“Mean Spearman is exactly 1.000” is contradicted two paragraphs later

§4.3 argues ρ̄ = 1.000 means “whenever two runs agree a feature is in the top‑8, they also agree on its precise rank” — then says “Rank 6 is occupied by sensor 3 in 4 runs and by sensor 7 in 1 run.” Both cannot be true. In Table 6, run 3 swaps S3 and S7, and both are in the intersection for pairs 2–3 and 3–4, giving ρ = 0.9643 for those two pairs and a mean of 0.9929.

Numerically small, but it falsifies the membership‑versus‑order dichotomy the section builds its interpretation on — and it is exactly what a referee checks with a pencil.

A structural problem with Eq. (16)

Spearman is computed on the intersection only, re‑ranked 1…q. That is close to guaranteed to return ≈1 here, for a reason unrelated to LIME’s stability: the features surviving into both top‑8 sets are overwhelmingly the high‑SNR ones, whose ordering is stable by construction. §4.3 says as much — ranks 1–5 are 100% stable — so restricting to the intersection discards precisely the features whose order is in doubt.

Computing Spearman over all p = 25 features on untruncated weight vectors gives ρ̄ = 0.849 for single‑run LIME — a number with actual resolution.

Where 0.636 sits §4.3

The obvious rejoinder to “I got 0.800, you got 0.636” is that both are estimates from five runs. So I measured the sampling distribution directly: 250 independent seeded LIME runs on instance #10, then 2,000 resamples of five, recomputing J̄ exactly as Eq. (15) prescribes.

In the paper’s favour: 0.636 is attainable. It is not fabricated, and a five‑run estimate genuinely can land there. My 0.800 is no more “correct” — both are single draws from a distribution with σ ≈ 0.05.

Against: it is the single most pessimistic estimate five runs can produce, below 99.25% of resamples. The headline therefore contrasts a tail‑minimum estimate of LIME against a point estimate of WA‑LIME. Since the true value is 0.741, the honest baseline is ≈0.74, and against it WA‑LIME’s 0.778 is a +5% gain, not +22.3%.

There is a clean fix: report J̄ with a confidence interval over many more runs. LIME takes ~0.02 s per explanation on this model, so it costs minutes of compute and converts the weakest claim in the paper into a defensible one.

The relative gain is quoted three different ways

0.778 / 0.636 = 1.2233, so +22.3%. The abstract and §4.4 say 22.3%; Table 8’s Change column says +22.4%; §5 opens with “A 22.4% stability gain”. Pick one — 22.3% is the correct rounding.

The Sensor-7 narrative does not survive re-running

§4.4 and the Conclusion both lean on it: “Sensor 7, which appears in only one of the five LIME runs (rank 6 in run 5 only), appears in all three WA‑LIME runs.”

Table 6 already contradicts it — it shows S7 in runs 2, 3 and 4 at ranks 7, 6 and 8, and not in run 5. The parenthetical matches no row of the paper’s own table. And in my re-run with the stated seeds, S7 appears in all five LIME runs, so there is no omission for WA‑LIME to correct.

§5 escalates this into an operational claim — that under standard LIME the HPC discharge stage “would have appeared in roughly one in five reports”. The measured rate is 68%. I would drop this narrative unless it survives a clean re-run.

A well-powered comparison on the paper’s own instance

Repeating the comparison on instance #10 with R = 100 runs per method — 4,950 pairs rather than 3 or 10 — on untruncated weight vectors:

WA‑LIME does work. Against a single LIME call it lifts Jaccard@8 from 0.820 to 0.937 and Spearman from 0.861 to 0.935. The paper’s qualitative claim is real, and this is a far stronger way to demonstrate it than 5‑versus‑3 runs.

But it does not beat the compute-matched baseline

Simply raising num_samples from 5,000 to 25,000 — one LIME call, no aggregation, no wrapper — reaches 0.9434 / 0.9507, edging out WA‑LIME on both metrics at identical cost. This holds on the paper’s own instance, and the same pattern appears across a separate 10‑instance study.

This is the objection most likely to sink the paper in review, because the baseline is obvious and cheap to run. The most promising response: aggregating over resampled backgrounds targets a variance component that extra perturbations cannot touch, which would give WA‑LIME a defensible niche. That claim needs its own experiment.

A note on k. LIME’s num_features is not a display truncation — it selects features and then refits the surrogate on that subset, so top‑8‑of‑8 and top‑8‑of‑25 are different quantities. Under the paper’s k = 8 protocol the true J̄ is 0.7409; untruncated it is 0.8201. Both are correct for their protocols; the paper should say which it used.

Editorial Pre-submission

  • An unresolved author note is still in the PDF, under Fig. 18 on p. 23: “(Can consider adding a statement on the increase of Spearman Rank Correlation/point to the results section for the same)”.
  • Broken cross-references. §4.4 says “In Figure 12, each curve plots the Jaccard index between the top‑8 sets at successive iterations” — that is Figure 18; Figure 12 is the third LIME iteration. §4.4 and §5 both cite “Section 3.3” for the five‑run instability test and the bimodal finding; both live in Section 4.3.
  • Garbled sentence in the abstract: “…increases the number of features retained in every run from 6 to 7 of the top‑8 set, and features that enter or leave the top‑k set across runs.” The final clause has lost its verb.
  • §4.2 ranking claim. “the least important as sensor measurement 19” — S19 appears in none of Table 6’s five runs, so this refers to the Figure 9 explanation only. Make that explicit.
  • Ranking rule mismatch. §3.6 says the top‑k is ranked by |βr · x̃r|; the notebook ranks by |βr| alone, which is what LIME’s as_list() returns. Same top‑6 here, but different quantities — describe the one the code computes. (Jaccard is identical either way; ρ̄ is 0.9857 by |β| vs 0.9976 by |β·x̃|.)
  • “S-LIME” in the notebook headings. S‑LIME (Zhou et al., KDD 2021) is a different algorithm using hypothesis testing for perturbation sizing. The paper correctly says WA‑LIME throughout; the released notebook should match.

What to do before submitting

  • Reconcile Tables 6 and 7. They describe different experiments. Re‑run the five stated seeds, regenerate both tables from that single run, and make the prose feature list match.
  • Replace the 5‑run point estimate with a properly powered one. Report J̄ over 50–100 runs with a confidence interval. Costs minutes.
  • Run the compute‑matched baseline and either re‑frame the contribution around it or find the regime where aggregation genuinely wins.
  • Fix ρ̄. Either compute it over all 25 features, or keep the intersection version as a diagnostic and drop the “membership, not order” conclusion.
  • Drop or re‑verify the Sensor‑7 narrative in §4.4, §5 and the Conclusion.
  • Sweep the editorial list above, starting with the author note on p. 23.
